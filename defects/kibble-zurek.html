Cooling too fast to keep up

Near a critical point a system relaxes ever more slowly, so any finite cooling rate eventually outruns it and the pattern freezes. Kibble and Zurek predicted how the frozen size depends on the rate. Here it is measured for walls, then for vortices, whose transition adds logarithms.

Warm-up: vortices and the Kosterlitz–Thouless transition

Before quenching anything, the engine has to get the equilibrium physics of the 2D XY model right. This model has no ordinary ordered phase. It still has a transition, at TKT ≈ 0.893, driven by vortex–antivortex pairs unbinding. This warm-up is finished.

How many walls a cooling leaves

The Kibble–Zurek argument in three measurements: how slowly the system relaxes near Tc (the exponent z), the size the pattern freezes at for each cooling rate, and what is left after the cooling ends.

Vortices: Kibble–Zurek with a logarithm

The second half of the project repeats the story for vortices, the 2D cousins of cosmic strings. Their transition (Kosterlitz–Thouless) has no ordinary critical exponent: the correlation length diverges exponentially, so the Kibble–Zurek counting picks up logarithms. Cooled through the transition at rates spanning a factor of 300, the vortices left behind fall roughly as τQ−0.7. But the apparent exponent steepens with slower cooling, 0.70 → 0.79, and for the slower ramps the data follow ln(τQ)/τQ better than any power law, as Jelić & Cugliandolo predicted. A pure log form doesn't fit the fastest ramps, though, which need an extra term.

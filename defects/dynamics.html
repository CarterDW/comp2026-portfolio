Defects · what happens next

After the quench

Once defects form they don't stay put: walls straighten and shrink, vortices find partners and annihilate. How fast depends on the dynamics, and the lattice models here show three different laws.

Code

Domain walls: the Ising quench

The Z₂ story starts with a faster engine: a checkerboard update of the Ising model at 100 million spin flips per second. It reproduces the exact Boltzmann distribution over all 65,536 states of a 4 × 4 lattice and the exact energy of a 64 × 64 lattice. One trap, caught by watching the movie rather than the equilibrium checks: with the usual Metropolis rule, a checkerboard flips every zero-cost spin of a sublattice at once, and the walls shrink too fast (t−0.8). The heat-bath rule fixes it and recovers the textbook t−1/2.

After the quench: how walls disappear

Once the walls exist, they coarsen. Under ordinary spin flips a wall moves to reduce its own curvature, so a single circular domain shrinks at a steady rate in area and vanishes in a time ∝ R², and the typical domain grows as t1/2. If the magnetization is conserved, as in an alloy separating, spins can only swap places, walls move only by carrying material along them, and growth slows to t1/3. Same start, same temperature, very different histories.

Vortices after the quench

Quench the spins below the transition and the vortices pair up and annihilate. Because the pull between a vortex and an antivortex weakens only logarithmically, and their drag grows with distance, the count falls as ln(t)/t rather than a clean power law, and a single planted pair takes a time ∝ R² ln R to meet. Both show up cleanly.
